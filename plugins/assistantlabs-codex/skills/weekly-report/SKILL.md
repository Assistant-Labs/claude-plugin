# The weekly report

Once a week, one document. It answers four things and stops: **what moved, what
is at risk, what needs you, what we do next.**

Read `reporting-to-the-owner` for the voice and the banned vocabulary. This
skill is about the weekly one specifically — what goes in it, where the numbers
come from, and how next week gets decided.

---

## Read the human half of the record, or the report will be wrong

This is the rule the whole skill exists for. **The agent's view is half the
relationship.** Calls, meetings and visits leave no trace in a conversation the
agent can see, and a weekly report built from conversations alone states, with
confidence and detail, things that are not true.

The shape of the failure, every time: a customer is reported as neglected when
the owner phoned them; a lead is ranked hot when they already bought elsewhere;
a meeting that happened is counted as never booked.

**Before writing a line about any customer or lead, read:**

1. **The messages the BUSINESS sent** — the owner's own replies in the thread,
   not the agent's. Those are the relationship.
2. **The notes on the contact record** — where calls get written up.
3. **The activity timeline**, if the CRM has one.
4. **The calendar**, for meetings — though a meeting arranged in chat often
   never reaches it, so absence proves nothing.

When something is in none of them, **ask the owner** rather than reporting the
gap as a fact. "Did you speak to anyone outside the app this week?" is one
sentence and it routinely changes the whole report.

---

## Counts, not rates — while the business is small

A percentage needs a denominator. With ten customers, one leaving is ten points
of churn, and two extra sales swing a conversion rate by a third. So:

- **No percentage on fewer than about 30 of anything.** Print the count.
- **Churn is a name and a sentence** — who left and why, in their words.
- **Never compare this week to last week** on something that moves by ones and
  twos. Show six weeks, or show nothing: a series has a shape, a single jump is
  a coin toss.
- **Refuse outright:** customer acquisition cost, lifetime value, retention rate,
  NPS. The numbers cannot carry them yet, and a confident false number is worse
  than none.

**Inputs before outputs.** By the time takings move, the thing that moved them
happened weeks ago. So lead with what they control — enquiries answered, jobs
booked, jobs actually done — and put money last.

## Ask them first — up to three questions, one at a time

**Some of the week only the owner knows.** Calls, visits, a deal agreed in
person, a customer who complained to their face — none of it reaches any system,
and a report written without it states things that are not true.

So before gathering anything, ask — **at most three, one at a time, with choices
they can tap**:

1. **What happened this week that the system would not know about?** Offer the
   shape, not the content: *"a couple of calls" / "a lot, I'll tell you" /
   "nothing"*.
2. **What should this week be measured against?** A target for the one number.
   Without it every figure is naked.
3. **What are you worried about?** Aims the digging at what they will read.

**Never ask what the data answers** — takings, messages handled, who is booked in.
Asking one of those wastes the question and reads as not having looked. Ask, wait,
let the answer change what you gather. If they do not answer, write the report
anyway and say which parts are unverified.

## Never window a lead list by time

A rolling "last 7 days" quietly deletes the oldest leads — which are the most
neglected, and therefore the most expensive ones to lose. A prospect who said
"yes, go ahead" a month ago and was never answered is the single most valuable
row in any weekly report, and a time window is exactly what hides them.

**List every open lead, and make age a column, not a filter.** Rank by who spoke
most recently — warmest first — but never drop a row for being old.

---

## The one number

Open with a single number, **the same one every week**, with the week's story
under it. It should be the one measure of whether the business is working —
bookings taken, jobs completed, new customers, whatever this business actually
sells. Everything else is supporting evidence.

It changes only when the business's goal changes, never because another number
looked better this week.

## What goes in

| | Section | Answers |
|---|---|---|
| — | **The top** | The one number · three or four facts · one sentence saying what the week means · **what needs them** |
| 1 | **Customers** | Who was served, who is unhappy, who went quiet |
| 2 | **Leads** | Everyone open, warmest first, with what each is waiting on |
| 3 | **The agent** | What it handled alone, where it was wrong, what changed |
| 4 | **Money** | In, outstanding, **at risk**, against target |
| 5 | **Next week** | Proposed work, to approve |

**And it opens with last week's commitments** — done or not done, no
explanations, before any number. A report with no memory of its own last edition
is a newsletter.

Assume they read the first page on a phone and nothing else. The rest exists so
any line on it can be checked.

---

## Every name is a tap

They read this on a phone. Naming someone they should ring and then making them
go and find the number has done half the job and left the expensive half undone.

**Link everything to where they would act on it** — a customer's name to a
`tel:` and a `wa.me/` link and their record, a job to the booking, a task to the
board, an invoice to the invoice. Use small icon chips on one line, not labelled
buttons, or a narrow column wraps and pushes rows off the page.

**Anything they must send comes written.** A customer owed a reply gets the
message drafted, ready to paste, in their own language. The gap between "chase
them" and chasing them is composing the sentence.

**One line at the top: if you only do one thing.** They may read nothing else.

---

## Decisions carry a default

Anything needing the owner says what happens **if they say nothing**:

> *Shall I message the eleven customers who haven't been back since spring?*
> If you don't reply by Sunday, I'll leave them alone.

Then silence is a valid answer, the business still moves, and they reply only to
disagree. **Two decisions at most.** A page of questions is a report that has
outsourced its thinking.

---

## Next week's work: builds, not busywork

Propose work as a short list with checkboxes. They reply `approve 1,2,4` and
those become tasks.

**A good task stops the problem happening again. A weak one handles today's
instance of it.**

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| Reply to the eight unanswered messages | Find why eight went unanswered and close that gap |
| Chase the customers who went quiet | Get told when a regular customer stops coming, before it costs a month |
| Fix this week's wrong answer | Teach the agent the fact it was missing, and check what else it does not know |

The test: **would this task be word-for-word identical next week?** If yes, the
real task is the build underneath it.

Aim at two things: **what their customers will need next**, and **what the
business needs to run itself with less effort.** A week of pure tidying is a
week that changed nothing.

Give every task enough that someone else could pick it up cold — what the
outcome is, what was measured, and what "done" looks like.

---

## Before sending

1. Did I ask them the off-system question before gathering?
2. Did I read what the **owner** said, not only the agent?
2. Is any list windowed by time? Widen it.
3. Can I re-derive every number today?
4. Does each item of next week's work stop something recurring?
5. Does everything needing them carry a default?
6. Would they understand every line without knowing how any of it is built?
